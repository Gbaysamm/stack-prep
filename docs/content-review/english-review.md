# Use of English lessons: content review

*Prepared 1 October 2026 for the founder and the external content expert. A read-only review: no
lesson was changed.*

## Executive summary

- **What was reviewed.** All 735 active UTME Use of English lessons (2010–2018) were measured
  against the new lesson style guide. The 30 lessons that scored hardest, two per topic, were then
  read in full. The 5 English model lessons were compared with their originals. 14 oral English
  lessons that use the talking mouth were also checked.
- **The main finding: the lessons are not too wordy. They are too compressed.**
  - The average lesson is 5.3 moments and about **67 spoken words**, roughly 30 seconds of voice.
  - 7 in 10 lessons have exactly five moments, whatever the question. A hard inference question
    gets the same five beats as an easy rhyme.
  - The sentences themselves are short (about 5–10 words), and the voice uses everyday words.
  - So the difficulty comes from what is *left out*, not from how it is said.
- **What is left out, across all 735 lessons:**
  - **No hook in 82%.** Most lessons open flatly: "We want the opposite of…", "We need the…".
  - **Traps not explained on the board in 31%.** At least one wrong option is never mentioned on
    the board. It gets one line of pop-up feedback, and only for a student who chose it. This is
    worst in comprehension: 76% of inference lessons and 44–47% of the others.
  - **Hard option words never defined in 29%.** Examples are *spurious*, *covertly*, *nonchalant*,
    *slothful* and *embodiment*. A student can follow why the answer is right and still not know
    what the other three words mean.
  - **Grammar or sound terms used without saying what they mean in 24%.** The commonest are
    *verb*, *stress*, *syllable*, *phrase*, *noun*, *rhyme*, *idiom*, *tag*, *plural*, *infer* and
    *clause*. This is concentrated in word stress (85%), concord and tense (85%) and grammar
    gap-fill (65%).
  - **A rule with no example in 18%.** "Positive statement, negative tag" is stated, but no
    familiar case is shown before it is used.
- **What already works:**
  - Every comprehension lesson marks the passage, and almost all quote it word for word.
  - Doodles are nearly everywhere outside oral English.
  - The talking mouth now covers all 117 oral lessons, and every option is voiced through it.
- **The model lessons are a big step forward.** They run 10–12 moments and 200–290 words, with a
  real hook, a definition of every term, and every option tested on its own line. Four things
  still need attention:
  - **Length:** single voice lines reach 30–35 words, which is long for a second-language listener
    on a phone.
  - **Hooks with no thinking time:** they ask "Can you spot it?" but give the student no moment to
    try.
  - **Two words slipped through undefined:** *literally* and *abound*.
  - **The model stress lesson doesn't use the talking mouth,** although every other oral lesson
    now does.
- **Two content-accuracy issues need the expert before any rewrite.**
  - **Printed keys.** 112 lessons (15%) teach a different answer from the printed key, mostly in
    cloze, synonyms, antonyms and inference. The team's reasoning is recorded for each one, but
    the expert should confirm them.
  - **Pronunciation.** A handful of oral lessons make claims that a phonetics specialist may
    dispute. Examples: "months is said 'munce'", "paths has the buzzing th", and "gear starts like
    ee".
- **Recommended rewrite order (difficulty × how often the topic appears on a paper):**
  1. Comprehension (87 lessons)
  2. Synonyms and antonyms (195)
  3. Idioms and sentence interpretation (86)
  4. Cloze (95)
  5. Grammar, concord and tense (70)
  6. Vocabulary and usage gap-fill (84)
  7. Word and emphatic stress (50)
  8. Vowels, consonants and rhymes (68)

  Start with a short pilot on real students, and gate every rewrite with the checklist in
  section 7. Rewrite each repeated question once (about 20 questions in 2016 repeat 2013 word for
  word).

## 1. How the lessons were measured

A script read every active lesson and its question, and recorded these signals. The script and
its full output (one row per lesson) are kept outside the repository, in the review scratch
folder, `review_en`.

| Signal | What it looks for |
|---|---|
| Length | Moments per lesson, words per voice line, sentences per moment, words per sentence |
| Reading level | A standard readability grade for the voice lines (sentence length and syllables) |
| Unexplained terms | A grammar or sound term (about 100 terms, e.g. *clause, concord, gerund, syllable, diphthong, idiom, infer*) where the first voice line using it, or the next, doesn't say what it means |
| Bare rules | A voice line that states a rule ("always", "takes a", "agrees with", "positive → negative") with no example beside it |
| Evidence | For passage and cloze questions: are there passage marks, and does the lesson quote the passage? |
| Traps on the board | Is each wrong option named or discussed somewhere in the lesson itself (not only in the pop-up feedback)? |
| Undefined option words | Is an uncommon word in the options or the target word ever given a meaning? |
| Hook | Does the first line open a question, a puzzle or a warning, rather than restate the task? |
| Hard words in the voice | Uncommon words that the *explanation* uses, apart from the question's own words |

The signals were turned into a **difficulty score from 0 to 100 for each lesson**. Each signal
was ranked against all lessons, then weighted: definitions, traps, evidence and terms count most.
The score is relative. The median lesson scores 26, and the top tenth score above 39.

**Caveats.**
- These are pattern checks, not judgements. They can miss a definition worded unusually, or count
  a trap as "addressed" when it is only named.
- Every one of the 30 lessons in section 4 was read by hand, and the hand reading agreed with the
  flags in all but a few places. Those places are noted in the table.
- The topics come from each question's syllabus code. Reading-text (set novel) questions are all
  retired, so they are not covered here.
- No active lessons are tagged for summary or for the spelling skill. Spelling items sit inside
  the vocabulary and usage gap-fill topic.

## 2. Findings across all English lessons

| Measure | All 735 lessons | The 5 model lessons |
|---|---|---|
| Moments per lesson | 5.3 (517 lessons have exactly 5; only 10 have more than 6) | 10–12 |
| Spoken words per lesson | about 67 (about 30 seconds) | 198–289 (about 1.5–2 minutes) |
| Words per voice line | 12.4 on average | 20–24 on average, up to 35 |
| Words per sentence | 5–10 (shortest in oral English) | 8–10 |
| Reading level of the voice | about grade 5 on average (easy) | about grade 3.5–5.5 |
| Opens with a hook | 18% | 100% |
| Every wrong option discussed on the board | 69% | 100% |
| Every hard option word defined | 71% | 4 of 5 (2014 Q7 leaves *abound* undefined) |
| Terms explained when first used | 76% | 100% (but see *literally*, section 5) |
| Titled sections and word-timed marks | 5 lessons (the models) | all |

**What this means.**
- **The voice is already plain.** Sentence length and vocabulary are not the problem. Very few
  uncommon words appear in the explanations. When they do, they are usually vivid ones (*swells*,
  *glides*, *buzzes*) or occasional slips: *momentum*, *literally*, *stance*, *praiseworthy*,
  *wavering*, *memorial*.
- **The lessons skip the middle.** A typical lesson is: task → one definition → answer → one
  line striking two options → answer again. A student who doesn't already half-know the word or
  rule has nothing to hold on to.
- **Trap teaching sits in the wrong place.** Almost every lesson (730 of 735) has a sentence for
  each wrong option, but only in the pop-up after a wrong pick. A student who guesses right never
  sees why the others fail. A student watching the lesson to learn sees one or two of the three.
- **The openings are formulaic.** The voice opens with "We want the…" 139 times and "We need
  the…" 44 times. Every lesson ends "…which is answer choice X." That ritual ending is fine, but
  the formulaic openings waste the most valuable moment.
- **Some questions repeat.** About 20 lexis and interpretation questions in 2016 repeat 2013 word
  for word, with near-identical lessons. 2014 Q51 and 2016 Q52 are the same antonym question.
  Rewrite each once, and copy it.
- **The answers need confirming.** 112 lessons teach an answer different from the printed key:
  cloze 23, synonyms 15, antonyms 11, inference 10, interpretation 10, vocabulary 10, and the rest
  spread out. The explanations files record why in each case. Many are probably errors in the
  printed key, but 15% is high enough that the expert should confirm them before lessons are
  rebuilt around them.

### What stands out in each topic

- **Inference, tone and attitude (hardest).**
  - The question words themselves are never taught: *infer*, *deduce*, *attitude*, *tone*.
  - The answer options are often attitude words that students don't know: *objective*,
    *non-committal*, *critical*, *indifferent*, *partial*, *covertly*, *nonchalant*. Only 28% of
    lessons define all of them.
  - Three in four leave a trap off the board. Yet "true in the passage but not what was asked" is
    the commonest UTME inference trap, and it is exactly the one that needs showing.
  - Tone has no visual language yet, though doodles of faces would suit it well.
- **Main idea and detail.** These are the best lessons by evidence: every lesson marks and quotes
  the passage. The gaps are the traps (44% leave one out) and abstract option words (*diminish*,
  *emancipation*, *slothful*). Title questions are taught well where present: the 2014 Q4
  "umbrella" covering both boxes is a good model.
- **Words and expressions in context.** The key move, "put each option back into the sentence and
  reread", is almost never shown. Planted traps (a word that appears nearby in the passage, like
  *recognition* in 2014 Q5) go unmentioned on the board. Figure-of-speech and "grammatical name"
  questions (2018 Q6) pile up terms.
- **Cloze.**
  - Clean, short lessons that point at the clue word.
  - Missing: reading the whole sentence and the one before it. Most lessons look only at the
    words touching the gap.
  - Also missing: the habit of checking grammar *and* meaning, and the final "read it back" check.
    The model 2014 Q16 shows all three.
- **Synonyms and antonyms (the largest group, 195 lessons).**
  - Every one opens flatly.
  - About 4 in 10 never discuss one of the wrong options, and about 1 in 3 leave a hard option
    word undefined.
  - Antonym lessons sometimes say "that is a synonym" without explaining the term. That is
    confusing in the very topic where students mix the two up.
  - The best lessons use a moving picture (the thirst meter in 2014 Q51), which is the right
    instinct.
- **Sentence interpretation and idioms.**
  - Good pictures and a clear literal-versus-real contrast.
  - But *idiom* is often used without being defined, and a third of lessons don't rule out every
    option on the board. Several explain one idiom with another (*let slip*, *on the verge*).
- **Grammar gap-fill, concord, tense and tags.**
  - These are the most term-heavy lessons: *possessive*, *plural*, *helping verb*,
    *present simple*, *participle*, *reported speech*.
  - Rules are stated before any example.
  - Some lessons assume an earlier one ("Question tag again.", "Same rule:").
  - These topics would gain most from a small table on the board.
- **Vocabulary and usage gap-fill.** Confusable pairs (*sited/sighted/cited*,
  *proscribed/prescribed*), prepositions, phrasal verbs and spelling. The lessons are short and
  mostly clear. The weak spot is again the wrong options: 3 in 10 leave one undiscussed.
- **Word stress.**
  - The -tion rule is well taught, with examples.
  - But *stress* and *syllable* are explained in only 4 lessons in 27. Several lessons say "Same
    rule…" as if the student had just watched another.
  - Lessons without a pattern (*valedictory*) give no rule at all, only "it follows words like…".
- **Emphatic stress.** None of the 23 lessons has a hook, and *emphatic stress* is rarely defined.
  The talking mouth says the stressed word on its own, but emphasis can only be heard against the
  rest of the sentence (section 5).
- **Vowels, consonants and rhymes.**
  - The easiest group, because the mouth carries them and every option is voiced.
  - The gaps:
    - **Sound symbols:** /ɪə/ and /ɔɪz/ are written without ever being introduced.
    - **Mixed-up terms:** *rhyme* is used to explain a vowel match, and *vowel* to explain a
      rhyme.
    - **Traps in a rush:** three traps are often run through in one moment.

**What the 30 have in common.**
1. **The wrong options are taught only in the pop-up.** Usually the most tempting wrong option
   is the one missing from the board.
2. **Hard words are explained with other hard words:** *wavering*, *memorial*, *quench*,
   *momentum*, *let slip*, *revolt*.
3. **One term goes undefined at the moment it matters:** *infer*, *possessive*, *syllable*,
   *clause*, *idiom*, *emphatic stress*.
4. **Some lessons assume an earlier one:** "Same rule", "again".
5. **Several reasons are packed into one moment.**

None of the 30 is hard because of long sentences.

## 5. Verdict on the model lessons and the talking-mouth lessons

### The five English model lessons

| Lesson | Before → after | What works | What is still hard or risky |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2014 Q7**, snakes: "most notable thing" | 7 → 12 moments | A real hook ("all four options are in the passage, so how do we choose?"). *Most notable* is defined. The passage is read paragraph by paragraph, with the cold-blooded *reason* now first. Every trap gets its own moment and a passage mark: B's "versatility is about where they live" is exactly the right kind of trap teaching. | It defines *most notable* as "the idea the passage spends most time on". That is a sound main-idea habit, but *notable* usually means "remarkable". The third paragraph (versatility, many families) is about as long as the eating material, and the printed key is A. **The expert should rule on this item before it is held up as a model.** *Abound* (option A) is never defined. Several voice lines run 30+ words. |
| **2014 Q16**, cloze "columnist" | 5 → 11 | The best of the five. It reads the sentence before the gap, shows "such a journalist" pointing back, builds the word (column + ist, like piano + ist), strikes each option on its own line, and reads the answer back into the gap. That is the complete cloze routine. | The hook asks "Can you spot it?" but the next moment answers at once. Give the student a beat to look (a pause, or a tap to continue). |
| **2014 Q38**, "pigs would fly" | 5 → 10 | *Idiom* is defined in plain words. The logic runs in two clean steps. Option A is ruled out as "leaves some hope", a lovely, exact reason. | *Literally* ("we don't take it literally") is used without explanation. For many second-language learners it is a harder word than *idiom*. "Remarked" is not explained either. |
| **2014 Q96**, "congratuLAtion" | 5 → 11 | *Stress* and *syllable* are defined, and the syllables are clapped. The rule is tested on three known words before use. The verb-versus-noun trap (conGRAtulate) is explained, which is real insight. A wrong example in the old lesson ("naTION") is fixed. | **It doesn't use the talking mouth**, though all 26 other word-stress lessons now do. It should be the model for the mouth as well. "It works almost every time" invites the question "when doesn't it?" (e.g. *television*); one honest sentence on exceptions would help. |
| **2011 Q42**, "corrugated" | 6 → 10 | A strong hook ("material or shape?"), which is the real decision in the question. A zinc roof is Nigerian and familiar. Each option fails for a *named kind* of reason (shape, material, look-alike). | "Both start with c, o, r" will be read letter by letter by the voice, which is fine, but check by ear. *Folded* is a loose synonym for *corrugated* (ridged), and the printed key says B; the expert may want to comment. |

**Overall verdict on the models.**
- **They meet the style guide, and they are much easier to follow.** Above all, every option is
  now taught where every student sees it, and every term is defined.
- **They are three to four times longer** (about 200–290 words against 67). That is right for
  hard items. It risks being tedious for easy ones: a student who knows that *beer* rhymes with
  *gear* doesn't need 11 moments.
- **Some voice lines are too long for listening in a second language.** The 30–35-word lines
  carry three or four sentences. A limit of about **25 words and two sentences per line** would
  keep the pace without losing content.
- **They are still passive.** No moment asks the student to predict or try before the reveal. One
  "Before I show you: which option would you strike first?" moment, with a pause, turns watching
  into practice.
- **They end on the answer, not on a habit.** A closing "next time" line would help students
  carry the method to new questions: "When you see *such a*, look back one sentence."
- **The template "Option A… Option B… Option C…" is clear but slow for vocabulary items.** For
  synonyms, antonyms and tone words, one picture that places all four options on a meaning line
  at once may teach more in fewer moments (section 6).

### The talking-mouth lessons (14 sampled)

Sampled across all five oral topics: 2012 Q99, 2013 Q98, 2015 Q100 (emphatic stress); 2011 Q95,
2014 Q95, 2015 Q97 (word stress); 2011 Q88, 2014 Q86, 2017 Q52 (vowels); 2010 Q92, 2015 Q90,
2016 Q92 (rhymes and sound matching); 2010 Q89, 2012 Q91 (consonants).

**What works.**
- **Every option is said by the mouth.** The lesson ends by saying the question word and the
  answer side by side ("Sheath, length: just air, no buzz."). That pairing is how
  minimal-pair teaching should end.
- **The descriptions are physical and easy to copy:** "top teeth on the lower lip, with a buzz",
  "lips pushed into a small round O, and held", "the tongue peeps out between the teeth". The
  "buzz / no buzz" test for voiced and voiceless sounds is excellent.
- **Tapping to replay slowly** gives the learner control, which is good practice for listening.
- **Stress lessons show the syllables under the mouth,** and the stressed one grows. That puts
  the beat on screen.

**What is still hard.**
- **Emphatic stress is the weak spot.**
  - The mouth says the stressed word in isolation ("ACCOUNTANT"), but emphasis only exists
    against the other words.
  - The student needs to hear the *whole sentence* twice, with the loud word moved, and see a
    table: loud word → the question it answers.
  - Whether the synthetic voice really puts more stress on a capitalised word is unverified, and
    capitalised names such as ASA may be spelled out letter by letter. **Someone needs to listen
    to all 23.**
- **The sound symbols appear without introduction.** /ɪə/, /ɔɪz/ and /əʊz/ are written on the
  board in most vowel and rhyme lessons. Either teach a small symbol set once, or replace the
  symbols with key words ("the ear sound, as in *here*").
- **Terms and accuracy.**
  - *Rhyme*, *vowel* and *syllable* are used to explain one another. Rhyme = the same vowel *and*
    ending, which is explained in some lessons but not others.
  - A few claims need a phonetician's check: "gear starts like ee", "months is said 'munce'",
    "paths has the buzzing th", "some say BAS-chun", and "valedictory follows dictionary".
- **Rules for word stress beyond -tion are missing.** Several lessons have no rule at all
  (*valedictory*: "it follows words like contradictory"). UTME uses a small number of
  dependable patterns that could each have their own rule card:
  - -tion/-sion, -ic, -ity and -ical put the stress on the syllable just before the ending;
  - -ee and -eer take the stress themselves;
  - two-syllable noun/verb pairs (REcord / reCORD);
  - compound nouns stress the first part.
- **Too much in some moments.** Three traps often share one moment. Most of the sampled lessons
  have no hook beyond "Watch my mouth". As a ritual, that opening is fine; as a hook, it is weak.

## 6. How to teach each topic to a struggling Nigerian student

The guidance draws on established reading and second-language practice:
- **Modelling aloud:** a teacher thinks out loud, then the student does it.
- **Question types** ("right there", "think and search", "author and me").
- **Explicit vocabulary teaching** with student-friendly definitions.
- **Context-clue types:** definition, restatement, contrast, example, general sense.
- **Word parts:** prefix, root, suffix.
- **Minimal pairs and physical cues** for sounds.
- **Low cognitive load:** one idea per moment, with the picture and the voice telling the same
  thing.
- **Contrast with Nigerian English and local languages,** where many traps come from.

Moment counts assume the 25-words-per-line limit suggested above.

### 6.1 Comprehension: main idea, detail and title

- **Strategy:**
  1. Read the question first and name its type ("This is a main-idea question").
  2. Underline the key words in the stem.
  3. Find those words, or their synonyms, in the passage, and mark the evidence.
  4. Say the evidence in your own words.
  5. Test every option against the evidence, and strike what fails.
  6. For main idea and title questions, ask "Does it cover the *whole* passage, or one part?"
- **UTME traps, and how to show them:**
  - **True but too narrow:** one paragraph's detail. Mark that one paragraph and label it "only
    here".
  - **Too wide:** goes beyond the passage. Strike it with "not in the passage".
  - **Same words, wrong meaning** (2014 Q7, B: *versatility*). Mark the word in the passage and
    write what it is really about.
  - **The source book's name offered as a title** (2014 Q4).
  - **Common knowledge that the passage doesn't say.**
- **Board tools:**
  - Passage marks for the evidence (highlight) and for each trap's origin (strike with a label).
  - A doodle that grows with each paragraph, like the snake's calendar and belly.
  - For titles, the "boxes and umbrella" doodle (2014 Q4).
  - A two-column live table (option | does the passage support it?) for detail questions with
    four close options.
- **Typical length:** 10–12 moments.

### 6.2 Comprehension: inference, tone, attitude and the writer's purpose

- **Strategy:**
  1. Teach the question words once, plainly. *Infer* or *deduce*: work out what the writer means
     but doesn't say. *Attitude* or *tone*: how the writer feels about the topic.
  2. Then: find two or three clues, say what they add up to, and pick the option that is
     supported but *not said word for word*.
  3. For tone, use a fixed bank of attitude words with faces, taught once and reused: *critical,
     objective, optimistic, pessimistic, indifferent, sarcastic, sympathetic, nostalgic,
     non-committal*.
- **UTME traps, and how to show them:**
  - **An option stated in the passage** (a fact, not an inference).
  - **An extreme word** (always, never, all).
  - **An attitude word near in meaning but wrong in strength:** *critical* vs *cynical*,
    *objective* vs *indifferent*.
  - **An option that is plausible in real life** but has no clue behind it.
  - Show each one with its clue, or its missing clue, marked in the passage.
- **Board tools:**
  - **Face doodles for tone,** placed on a live number line from negative to positive, with the
    four options set on it. The voice moves the marker as each clue is read.
  - Passage marks: a highlight on each clue, numbered 1, 2, 3.
  - A doodle that "adds up" the clues: three arrows into one conclusion.
- **Typical length:** 10–13 moments. These are the hardest items and deserve the most.

### 6.3 Comprehension: words and expressions in context

- **Strategy:**
  1. Find the word in the passage and read the *whole* sentence, plus the one before.
  2. Find the context clue and name its type (a definition, a contrast word like *but*, an
     example, a pair like "alteration or transformation").
  3. Guess a plain meaning *before* looking at the options.
  4. Put each option in place of the word and reread: does the sentence still mean the same?
- **UTME traps:**
  - **The dictionary meaning that doesn't fit this passage.** Show both meanings, then which one
    the sentence uses.
  - **A word planted nearby** (2014 Q5, *recognition*).
  - **A look-alike word.**
  - For figure-of-speech and "grammatical name" items: an answer that is true but too general.
- **Board tools:**
  - Passage marks: the target circled, the clue highlighted, and an arrow between them.
  - A substitution line on the board for each option ("…in human behaviour" with each option
    dropped in, struck or ticked).
  - For grammar-name items, a small live tree diagram (clause → main / subordinate → relative /
    adverbial).
- **Typical length:** 8–10 moments.

### 6.4 Cloze

- **Strategy:**
  1. Read the whole passage once for its topic; the lesson can say it in one line.
  2. At the gap, read the whole sentence and the one before.
  3. Ask two questions: what *kind* of word fits here (a thing, an action, a describing word),
     and what *meaning* does the passage need?
  4. Look for collocations, words that go together (*volcanic eruption*, *pose a danger*).
  5. Read the answer back into the sentence.
- **UTME traps:**
  - **Right meaning, wrong form** (*infection* for *infections*).
  - **Right grammar, wrong meaning.**
  - **A word that fits the sentence but not the passage's topic.**
  - **Near-synonyms with different strength or register** (*slang*, *dialect* or *language* for a
    formal speech).
- **Board tools:**
  - A passage highlight on the clue words on *both* sides of the gap.
  - A "pointing back" arrow for words such as *such*, *this* and *it*.
  - Word-building on the board (column + ist).
  - A small doodle for the meaning (the volcano).
  - The read-back moment with the answer dropped into the passage.
- **Typical length:** 8–10 moments. The model 2014 Q16 is the template.

### 6.5 Synonyms (nearest in meaning)

- **Strategy:**
  1. Read the sentence for a clue to the meaning.
  2. Give a student-friendly definition of the target in under 8 everyday words.
  3. Give every option its own short definition.
  4. Choose the option that matches in meaning, in **strength**, and in **part of speech**.
  5. Break long words into parts (*in-extinguish-able*: not + put out + can be).
- **UTME traps:**
  - **The opposite word,** which students grab when they half-know the target.
  - **A word from the same topic but a different meaning** (*epitaph* / *book*).
  - **A near-synonym of the wrong strength** (*shimmer* / *beam*).
  - **A look-alike** (*corrugated* / *corrupted*).
  - **An option that fits the sentence but doesn't mean the word.** That is the commonest trap:
    show the student that "it makes sense in the sentence" is not the test.
- **Board tools:**
  - A **meaning line** (a live number line or a doodle scale) with the target marked and all four
    options placed at once: same / close / different / opposite.
  - A doodle that acts out the meaning: the thirst meter, the corrugated roof.
- **Typical length:** 7–9 moments.

### 6.6 Antonyms (opposite in meaning)

- **Strategy:** the same as synonyms, with one extra step. First find the target's meaning,
  *then* say its opposite in plain words, *then* look for that among the options. Keep the word
  "synonym" out until it has been taught ("means nearly the same").
- **UTME traps:**
  - **The synonym placed as bait.** In almost every item, one option means the *same* as the
    target. Make this the standing hook for the topic: "One option here is a trap that means the
    same. Spot it."
  - **The negative-prefix look-alike** (*unquenchable* → *inextinguishable*).
  - **An opposite of a different meaning of the word.**
  - **The sentence-context trap:** the sentence often makes the original word sound odd ("the
    laureate's activity is *heinous*"), and students pick whatever fits the sentence. Teach them
    to test the word, not the sentence.
- **Board tools:** the meaning line from 6.5, with an arrow flipping from the target to the
  opposite end. A doodle that reverses (the meter fills, then drops).
- **Typical length:** 7–9 moments.

### 6.7 Sentence interpretation and idioms

- **Strategy:**
  1. Define *idiom* once: the words together mean something different from each word alone.
  2. Picture the literal meaning (it is usually funny), then say the real meaning.
  3. Use the sentence around the idiom to confirm it.
  4. For sentence interpretation without an idiom, rephrase the sentence in simple words first,
     then compare.
- **UTME traps:**
  - **The literal reading** (a brush from Spain).
  - **The half-right reading:** the right topic, the wrong degree, as in "if she worked harder"
    versus "never".
  - **A meaning flipped by a negative or a hedge word.**
  - **An option that is true in life but not said.**
- **Board tools:** a two-panel doodle, literal (crossed out) against real, with the person from
  the sentence (Jummai, Olatinuke) acted on. Strike each option on its own line with a named
  reason: *too literal*, *not in the sentence*, *too strong* or *too weak*.
- **Typical length:** 8–10 moments.

### 6.8 Vocabulary and usage gap-fill (word choice, confusables, prepositions, phrasal verbs, spelling)

- **Strategy:**
  1. Read the whole sentence and find the clue (*plane* → *sighted*).
  2. For confusable sets, give each word its own picture or use (*site* = a place, *cite* =
     quote, *sight* = see).
  3. For prepositions and phrasal verbs, teach the pairing as a fixed phrase
     ("sent **into** exile").
  4. For spelling, break the word into parts and show the rule (*eclectic*).
- **UTME traps:**
  - **Sound-alikes** (homophones).
  - **Look-alikes** (*proscribed* / *prescribed*, *satiated* / *saturated*).
  - **Phrasal verbs with the opposite effect** (*mopped up*).
  - **Nigerian English habits that look right:** *discuss about*, *voice out*, *advices*,
    *furnitures*.
- **Board tools:** a small live table of the confusable set (word | meaning | example), a doodle
  per meaning, and strikes on each wrong form.
- **Typical length:** 7–9 moments.

### 6.9 Grammar gap-fill (sentence patterns)

- **Strategy:**
  1. Read the sentence and find what the gap must connect to: its subject, the word it describes,
     or the idea before it.
  2. Name the pattern in plain words first, and use the grammar term only after an example
     ("*few* is for things you can count, like friends").
  3. Test each option in the gap.
- **UTME traps:**
  - **Pairs of near-equal forms** (*few* / *a few* / *little*; *indeed* / *however*).
  - **An idea in the middle of the sentence that misleads** ("the employer, not the salesmen,
    ___").
  - **A form that is correct in Nigerian speech but not in standard English.**
  - **Apostrophes on plurals.**
- **Board tools:**
  - A **live table** for the pattern: countable vs uncountable, singular vs plural.
  - Arrows from the gap to the word that governs it.
  - The middle phrase greyed out ("not the salesmen") to show what the verb really agrees with.
- **Typical length:** 8–10 moments.

### 6.10 Concord, tense, question tags and word classes

- **Strategy:**
  1. **Concord:** find the true subject (cross out "of the women", "together with…"), decide
     one or many, then match the verb.
  2. **Tense:** put the time words on a timeline.
  3. **Question tags:** statement → its helping verb → flip positive or negative → the same
     person.
  4. **Word classes:** ask what job the word is doing in *this* sentence.
- **UTME traps:**
  - "One of the … who" (*sells* or *sell*).
  - *Each*, *every* and *neither* with a plural noun nearby.
  - Mixing the past and the present.
  - **The Nigerian all-purpose tag "isn't it?"**. Name it directly; students will recognise it.
  - "Not only … but also" word order.
- **Board tools:**
  - **Live tables are ideal here.** A tag table (statement | helping verb | flip | tag); a concord
    table (subject | one or many? | verb).
  - A **timeline** (a live number line marked past / now / future) for tense.
  - Strikes on each option with the row of the table it breaks.
- **Typical length:** 8–10 moments.

### 6.11 Vowels

- **Strategy:**
  1. Say the target word, and isolate the sound.
  2. Give a well-known key word with the same sound (*see*, *food*, *here*).
  3. Describe the mouth (spread or round, long or short, sliding or still).
  4. Test each option by saying it in the same mouth.
  5. Spelling lies: the same letters can make different sounds (*feature* / *gear*).
- **UTME traps, especially for Nigerian speakers:**
  - **Long vs short pairs** (/iː/ vs /ɪ/: *sheep* / *ship*; /uː/ vs /ʊ/: *cool* / *full*). Many
    Nigerian languages don't make these distinctions.
  - **Diphthongs said as single vowels** (/eɪ/ as /e/, /əʊ/ as /o/).
  - The *ea* and *oo* spellings.
  - **Silent or unusual letters** (*colonel*).
- **Board tools:**
  - **The talking mouth,** with comparing moments (`say` on the same mouth) for the target, the
    answer and each trap.
  - A short or long bar under each word for length.
  - The tap-to-replay prompt said aloud once ("tap my mouth to hear it again").
- **Typical length:** 7–8 moments.

### 6.12 Consonants

- **Strategy:**
  1. Isolate the sound.
  2. Name *where* it is made (lips, teeth, tongue tip) and whether it **buzzes**: hand on the
     throat.
  3. Compare it with its partner sound (θ/ð, s/z, f/v, ʃ/tʃ).
  4. Test each option.
- **UTME traps:**
  - **The same spelling, a different sound** (*th* in *bathe* and *sheath*; *ch* in *chef* and
    *church*).
  - **The *-tion* and *-sion* sounds.**
  - **Silent letters.**
  - **Sounds many Nigerian speakers replace:** /θ/ → /t/, /ð/ → /d/, /v/ → /f/ in some regions,
    /ʒ/ → /ʃ/.
- **Board tools:** the mouth with a `side` view for tongue sounds, a "buzz" doodle, and a
  hand-on-throat instruction in the voice.
- **Typical length:** 7–8 moments.

### 6.13 Rhymes and homophones

- **Strategy:**
  1. Define *rhyme* once: the same last vowel *and* the same sound after it.
  2. Cut the word at its last vowel (b-**oys**), and test each option's ending by ear, not by
     spelling.
  3. For homophones: the same sound, a different spelling and meaning.
- **UTME traps:**
  - **A spelling twin that sounds different** (*ever* / *fever*).
  - **The same vowel with a different ending** (*boys* / *moist*).
  - **The same ending with a different vowel** (*stays*).
  - **An extra syllable** (*heavier*).
- **Board tools:** the mouth saying pairs, the "bells ring the same note" doodle (already used
  well), and the rhyme part underlined in each option.
- **Typical length:** 7–8 moments.

### 6.14 Word stress

- **Strategy:**
  1. Define *stress* (one syllable louder and longer) and *syllable* (a beat; clap it).
  2. Count the beats.
  3. Look for a pattern rule: an ending (-tion, -ic, -ity), a prefix, a noun/verb pair, or a
     compound.
  4. Test the rule on two known words.
  5. Apply it, then rule out each option.
- **UTME traps:**
  - **The stress of the related verb or root** (conGRAtulate, MOtive).
  - **Stressing the ending itself** (-TION).
  - **Nigerian English stress** that differs from the standard (e.g. *CERtificate* vs
    *cerTIficate*, *poLICE* vs *POlice*-type shifts). Name these explicitly.
- **Board tools:** the mouth with `syllables` and `stress` (the beat grows), a clap doodle, and
  a small rule card per ending, reused across lessons.
- **Typical length:** 8–10 moments. 2014 Q96 is the template, once the mouth is added.

### 6.15 Emphatic stress

- **Strategy:**
  1. Define it once: the speaker makes one word louder to correct or answer something.
  2. Ask what that word answers:
     - a person → *who?*;
     - a thing → *what?*;
     - a time → *when?*;
     - a place → *where?*;
     - a verb → was it this action or another?
  3. The right option is the question that this loud word answers, often by correcting a wrong
     guess ("Did the *cashier* pay?" → "No, the ACCOUNTANT did").
- **UTME traps:**
  - **A question about another part of the sentence.**
  - **A question that already contains the stressed word's information.**
  - **Options that are statements with a question mark.**
- **Board tools:**
  - The **whole sentence spoken twice** with different loud words, the loud word growing on the
    board. The mouth should say the sentence, not the single word.
  - A **live table**: loud word → question word → matching option.
  - A face doodle for "correcting" (a raised finger).
- **Typical length:** 7–9 moments.

## 7. Plan for rewriting all English lessons

### 7.1 Before rewriting: agree the foundations (about 1 week)

1. **The expert confirms the topic guides in section 6,** and the decisions in section 8.
2. **Build three shared banks, used by every lesson:**
   - **Key terms:** about 30, each with a one-line plain definition and an example: *syllable,
     stress, vowel, rhyme, idiom, infer, attitude, noun, verb, plural, tag, clause*…
   - **Student-friendly definitions** for every uncommon option word in the bank: about 600
     words, each in 8 everyday words or fewer.
   - **Tone and attitude words,** each with a face.
3. **Clear the answer list.** The expert reviews the 112 key disagreements (section 2), starting
   with the comprehension and cloze ones.

### 7.2 Pilot (about 1–2 weeks)

- Rewrite **10 lessons in each of three topics** (comprehension, antonyms, emphatic stress) to
  the guide.
- Test them with **5–10 real students** on phones. Each student watches a lesson, then answers a
  *fresh* question of the same type. Ask them to say where they got lost.
- Use the results to fix the length limits (moments, words per line) before scaling up.

### 7.3 Rewrite order

Ordered by difficulty × frequency, with quick wins grouped where the fix is formulaic.

| Wave | Topic | Lessons | Why this order | Target moments | Main fixes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Comprehension (main idea, inference, words in context) | 87 | Hardest group and the most marks; traps worst | 10–13 | Name the question type; evidence for answer *and* traps; tone faces; substitution test |
| 2 | Antonyms and synonyms | 195 | The most lessons; the fix is a repeatable pattern | 7–9 | Hook on the bait option; define all four options; meaning line |
| 3 | Sentence interpretation and idioms | 86 | High frequency; literal-versus-real is quick to add | 8–10 | Define *idiom*; strike every option with a named reason |

| 5 | Grammar gap-fill; concord, tense and tags | 70 | The most term-heavy; needs the term bank first | 8–10 | Tables; example before term; no "again" |
| 6 | Vocabulary and usage gap-fill | 84 | Lower difficulty; similar fix to wave 2 | 7–9 | Confusable tables; every option defined |
| 7 | Word stress; emphatic stress | 50 | Hard, but small, and the mouth is already in | 8–10 | Define the terms; rule cards; whole-sentence emphasis; check the audio |

**Repeated questions** (about 20 in 2016 that repeat 2013, plus 2014 Q51 / 2016 Q52) are
rewritten once and copied.

### 7.4 Checks for every rewritten lesson

**Automatic checks:** run the same measurements as this review; a lesson should pass before human
review.
- Every wrong option is discussed on the board.
- Every uncommon option word and the target word are defined.
- Every term from the term bank is defined at its first use in the lesson, or linked to the bank.
- A hook is present.
- No voice line is over about 25 words or 2 sentences.
- The moment count is in the topic's range.
- Passage questions mark the evidence for the answer and for every trap that comes from the
  passage.
- No "again" or "same rule" that assumes another lesson.
- The answer agrees with the explanations file.

**Human review:**
- **The writer, first:**
  - **Coverage:** walk the style-guide checklist plus the English additions above.
  - **Listen once, at real speed, on a phone.**
- **A second reader:**
  - **Every comprehension and inference lesson** (100%).
  - **A 20% sample** of every other topic.
  - **All emphatic-stress lessons, by ear,** to confirm the voice really stresses the word and
    doesn't spell capitals.
- **The content expert:**
  - Signs off the topic templates.
  - Checks every key disagreement.
  - Checks every pronunciation claim flagged in section 5.
  - Checks a random 5% of finished lessons.

## 8. Open questions for the content expert

1. **Printed keys.** 112 English lessons teach an answer different from the printed key, with the
   team's reasoning recorded for each. Which should we keep? Are some printed keys known to be
   reliable for certain years?
2. **2014 Q7 ("the most notable thing about snakes").** Is "the idea the passage spends most time
   on" a fair reading of *most notable*? Is C or A the defensible answer? And should a model
   lesson use an item this contested?
3. **2013 Q57 (epitaph → monument) and 2011 Q42 (corrugated → folded).** Are these keys
   defensible, or should these items be retired?
4. **Sound symbols.** Should lessons teach the phonetic symbols (/ɪə/, /θ/…), as some SS3
   teachers and WAEC do, or only key words ("the sound in *here*")? If symbols, which minimum set?
5. **Pronunciation standard.** JAMB's keys follow a British standard. How should lessons treat
   common Nigerian English pronunciations and stress: name them as the trap, or stay silent?
   Please check the specific claims: *months* without th, *paths* with the buzzing th, *gear*
   "starting like ee", *bastion* as "BAS-chun", *valedictory* stressed like *dictionary*.
6. **Terms.** Which grammar terms must a UTME candidate *know* (e.g. relative clause,
   non-defining, participle), and which should lessons avoid altogether by using plain
   descriptions?
7. **Length against attention.** The models run 10–12 moments (about 1.5–2 minutes). For easy
   items such as rhymes and simple synonyms, would you accept a shorter version (7–8 moments),
   with an optional "show me every option" extension?
8. **Active moments.** Should lessons include a "try it first" pause before the reveal, and a
   closing "habit to keep" line? Would these help weak students or slow them down?
9. **Tone and attitude words.** Can you give, or approve, a list of the attitude words UTME
   actually uses, so we can teach them once with faces and reuse them?
10. **Emphatic stress.** Is the "loud word → the question it answers or corrects" method the one
    you would teach? Are there UTME items where it fails?
11. **Antonym sentences.** Many UTME antonym items put the word in a sentence where the opposite
    makes more sense. Should lessons teach "ignore the sentence; test the word", or does that
    mislead on some items?
12. **Retired material.** All set-novel (reading-text) questions are retired. Should comprehension
    lessons point students to the current set text, and should a general "how to answer
    reading-text questions" lesson be written?
