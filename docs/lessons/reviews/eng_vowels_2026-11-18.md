# Vowels pilot: the founder's review (18 Nov 2026)

On `eng_vowels_1.yaml` (Pure vowels), first version. Score 7.5/10: good structure, relevant
exam questions; the teaching needs work. The general rules that came out of it are in
[the lesson guide](../subtopic-lesson-guide.md), "English sound lessons".

## The new objective (founder)

The lesson must teach the student **how to identify** short and long vowel sounds (and which
sound exactly), even in a word they have never met, not how to pronounce them. English spelling
is unpredictable: words that look alike can sound different, and words that look different
can share a sound. We will not add a pronunciation map or audio, so every pronunciation
demonstration goes. If a student can tell which sound a word has, saying it follows.

The reviewer's adjustment: knowing "long or short" is not enough; there are several of each,
so the student must name the exact sound. And there is no universal spelling rule: give a
reliable exam method, useful spelling clues (with their limits) and fixed key words.

Proposed order: why spelling misleads; short and long sounds and the length mark; the
twelve sounds, gradually, with key words; spelling clues and their limits; the identification
method; guided exam examples; independent practice.

Spelling-versus-sound examples to use: cat / car (same letter, different sounds); see / sea
(different spelling, same sound); food / good (same letters, different sounds); sit / seat.
Clues: final silent e (bit → bite, hop → hope, cut → cute), common vowel pairs (see, boat,
rain, team; but book and food differ), one letter, many sounds (hat, hate, father).

## Line by line

- **Line 2:** state the model: "English has only five vowel letters, A, E, I, O and U, but in
  the British English pronunciation model we are studying, there are twenty vowel sounds. This
  means the spelling of a word does not always tell us how its vowel is pronounced."
- **Line 4:** "stay in one position" is too absolute: "a vowel sound produced without the
  tongue changing position significantly". Board: "one steady vowel sound (no movement from
  one vowel to another)".
- **Line 6:** not "a colon": "We write long vowel sounds with a special length mark after their
  symbols. It looks like two small dots, as in /iː/."
- **Lines 7–12 (weakest part):** twelve sounds in six moments, almost no explanation. Give
  each pair time: "Listen carefully to the difference between the vowel sounds in sit and seat.
  The vowel in sit is /ɪ/, while the vowel in seat is /iː/." After the first pair, say that
  short and long differ in quality too, not only length ("their actual sounds are also slightly
  different, so we use different symbols"). Never teach "listen for how long it lasts" alone.
- **Line 11:** schwa is NOT the short partner of /ɜː/. "Now the vowel in bird, /ɜː/, a long
  vowel. Another central vowel is /ə/, called schwa, heard at the beginning of about. Schwa is
  a weak, unstressed vowel and the most common vowel sound in English."
- **Line 13:** prepare the task: "Now that we have learnt the twelve pure vowels and their key
  words, let's see how JAMB tests this knowledge. Consider this question from 2018. The word is
  key, and the letters 'ey' are marked. Which of the following words has the same vowel sound?"
  Then the options.
- **Line 15:** explicit, one option at a time: "Sit contains /ɪ/, the short vowel in sit. Bet
  and tread both contain /e/, the vowel sound in bed. Neither matches the sound in key." The
  board must keep the options readable, not lumped.
- **Line 17:** no generalisation about Nigerian speakers: "One common mistake is confusing
  similar vowel sounds. Some learners pronounce sit and seat almost the same way, or confuse
  full with fool. But these words have different vowel sounds." End on "listen to the vowel
  quality and length", not only "short or long".
- **Line 19:** method, then each option: "First, let's identify the vowel in bubble. It is the
  vowel sound we hear in cup, /ʌ/. Bull has the vowel sound in book, while gurgle has the vowel
  sound in bird. Guy contains a different type of vowel sound, which we will study in the next
  lesson." Board: each word with its symbol (bubble /ʌ/, bull /ʊ/, gurgle /ɜː/, guy /aɪ/).
- **Recap:** reinforce the skill: "English has twelve pure vowel sounds in the model we have
  studied, seven short and five long, each with its own symbol and a key word. To answer, find
  the vowel sound in the given word, recall its key word, and compare it with the options.
  Words can have different spellings but the same vowel sound."
- **Problem:** patch → mad is right in the British model. Underline the letter a in patch;
  never write "p-a-tch".
